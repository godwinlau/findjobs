**Step 1: What are you looking for?**
Single select: "Full-time job", "Freelance/Contract", "Internship", "Just browsing"

This is the cheapest possible first question — one tap, zero typing — and it immediately segments the user so every subsequent screen can be contextual. An intern sees different copy and skill suggestions than a senior freelancer.

**Step 2: Pick your skills**
The screen we just built. Flat grid, tap to select, 15 seconds. Pre-sorted by PH market demand. Custom input at bottom.

This is the single most important data point for matching. It comes second (not first) because Step 1 warms the user up with an effortless single-tap question. By the time they hit the skill grid they're already in "selection mode."

**Step 3: Job preferences**
Three quick inputs on one screen: Role titles (multi-select pills like "Product Designer", "Frontend Dev", "UX Engineer"), Location preference (single select: "Remote", "On-site", "Hybrid", "No preference"), and Salary expectation (optional slider or range picker, clearly marked "optional — helps us filter").

Salary being optional is key — it's a sensitive question in PH culture. Making it required would cause drop-off. But the users who do fill it in get noticeably better matches, which creates organic word-of-mouth ("the app actually shows jobs in my range").

**Step 4: Your matches are ready**
Not a form. This is the payoff screen. Show 3–5 real matched jobs immediately with match percentages. A headline like "We found 32 jobs for you" with a CTA: "View your matches →". This is where the dopamine hits and the user understands why they just answered 3 questions.

No profile photo. No bio. No resume upload. No self-assessment. All of that comes later through progressive profiling — gentle prompts on the home screen like "Add a photo to stand out to employers" or "Rate your React proficiency to improve match accuracy."

**Why this order works:**
- Step 1 (1 tap) → Step 2 (5–8 taps) → Step 3 (3–4 taps) → Step 4 (reward). Total time: under 60 seconds. Each step escalates effort slightly but never crosses the threshold where a user thinks "this isn't worth it." And the final step isn't a form — it's a gift.
